# Reviewer behaviour and LLM review simulation: literature notes

Compiled 2026-10-13 for the harness.
Purpose: ground the simulated reviewer personas, the AC/discussion phase, and the writer-vs-reviewer loop in published evidence.

**Labels**
- `VERIFIED [full]`: I read the full text (HTML, PDF text, or full web page) and the numbers come from it.
- `VERIFIED [abs]`: I read the official abstract or landing page; the numbers come from it. Details beyond the abstract are not checked.
- `SECONDARY`: the claim comes from a search summary, press report, or another paper's summary. Re-check before quoting it in anything public.
- `Inference:` marks my own reading of a result. It is not a claim the cited authors make.

## 1. Reviewer archetypes and biases

### 1.1 Simulated archetypes (the persona axes)
- **AgentReview** (Jin, Zhao, Wang, Chen, Zhu, Xiao, Wang; EMNLP 2024 oral; https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12708). `VERIFIED [full]`
  - Persona axes: **commitment** (responsible / irresponsible), **intention** (benign / malicious), **knowledgeability** (knowledgeable / unknowledgeable); AC styles: **inclusive, conformist, authoritarian**. Personas are fixed prompt descriptions.
  - Reviewer biases account for a 37.1% variation in decisions (abstract figure; how it was computed is not given in the text I read).
  - One irresponsible reviewer lowers the effort of the others: post-discussion review length fell 18.7% (195.5 to 159.0 words), and the mean rating fell 0.25 (5.256 to 5.005).
  - Going from 0 to 3 malicious reviewers drops the mean rating from 5.11 to 3.35. One or two malicious reviewers also pull the benign reviewers down by 0.14 / 0.10 (spill-over). Malicious reviewers cite "lack of novelty" in 10.4% of feedback, against 3.69% for benign ones.
  - Unknowledgeable reviewers are 24% more likely to cite insufficient discussion of limitations; experts give 6.8% more critiques of experimental validation.
  - AC style vs baseline decisions: inclusive kappa 0.578 (82.4% agreement), conformist 0.569 (82.0%), authoritarian 0.266 (~69%).
  - Authority bias: when 1, 2, or 3 reviewers know the authors, the Jaccard overlap of accepted lower-quality papers with the baseline is 0.364, 0.154, 0.008. When all reviewers know the authors of 10% of papers, decisions change by 27.7% (introduction figure).
  - Removing the rebuttal changes little (kappa 0.668, 86.1% agreement); the authors attribute this to anchoring. Discussion cuts score SD by 27.2% (0.224 to 0.163).
- **Paper champions vs paper killers** (Jim Kajiya, "How to Get Your SIGGRAPH Paper Rejected", SIGGRAPH 93 Papers Chair; https://ics-websites.science.uu.nl/docs/vakken/mcrws/xtra/kajiya.pdf). `VERIFIED [full]` Some committee members "ferret out the good features" and argue for acceptance (champions, Sederberg's term). Others are "very articulate, forceful, and negative": they "look for and find flaws" and sway the committee to reject (killers, Catmull's term).
- **"Reviewer 2"** (Peterson, "Dear Reviewer 2: Go F' Yourself", *Social Science Quarterly* 101(4):1648-1652, 2020; https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/socsci/v101y2020i4p1648-1652.html). `VERIFIED [abs]` Using the *Political Behavior* reviewer database, Reviewer 2 is neither more negative nor an outlier. Reviewer 3 is the one more often more than one category below the other reviewers. Takeaway for the harness: the "harsh outlier" is a real role, but it is not tied to a slot number.
- **CV folklore archetypes** (Ming-Hsuan Yang, "How to Get Your CVPR Paper Rejected?" slides, c. 2015-16; https://vision.sjtu.edu.cn/files/How-to-get-your-CVPR-paper-rejected.pdf). `VERIFIED [full]` "Me: Here is a faster horse / R1: You should have used my donkey / R2: This is not a horse, it's a mule / R3: I want a unicorn!" These map to the "my preferred method", "category error / misread", and "unbounded demands" reviewer types.

### 1.2 Consistency and arbitrariness
- **NIPS 2014 experiment** (Eric Price, guest post "The NIPS experiment", Moody Rd blog, 2014; http://blog.mrtz.org/2014/12/15/the-nips-experiment.html). `VERIFIED [full]` 166 papers were reviewed by two independent committees, which disagreed on 43 (25.9%). About 57% of accepted papers (95% CI 40-75%) would be rejected on a rerun. A purely random committee would give 77.5%, and people had expected about 30%.
- **Cortes & Lawrence**, "Inconsistency in Conference Peer Review: Revisiting the 2014 NeurIPS Experiment" (arXiv 2021; https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.09774). `VERIFIED [abs]` About 50% of the variation in reviewer quality scores is subjective. For accepted papers, scores do not correlate with later citation impact. For rejected papers later published elsewhere, they do.
- **NeurIPS 2021 consistency experiment** (Beygelzimer, Dauphin, Liang, Wortman Vaughan; blog https://blog.neurips.cc/2021/12/08/the-neurips-2021-consistency-experiment/ and arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03262). `VERIFIED [full blog]` 882 duplicated papers; the committees disagreed on 23.0% (random baseline 35.1%). 50.6% of papers accepted by one committee were rejected by the other, and 14.9% of rejected papers were accepted by the other. Orals and spotlights: more than half of spotlights picked by either committee were rejected by the other (13/25, 13/23). The arXiv abstract adds that making the conference more selective would increase arbitrariness.
- **ICLR 2017-2020** (Tran, Valtchanov, Ganapathy, Feng, Slud, Goldblum, Goldstein, "An Open Review of OpenReview", arXiv 2020; https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05137). `VERIFIED [full]` Estimated reproducibility of acceptance: 75% (2017), 70% (2018, 2019), 66% (2020); Computer Vision topic 59% in 2020. Reproducibility tops out at 75% even with infinitely many reviewers, because AC standards vary. Reviewer SD is 1.72 against a paper-quality SD of 2.95. Krippendorff's alpha fell from 0.56 (2017) to 0.39 (2020). Score-citation Spearman is about 0 for orals (-0.006) and spotlights (-0.043).
- **ACL 2023 PC report** (Rogers, Karpinska, Boyd-Graber, Okazaki; https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.911, blog https://2023.aclweb.org/blog/review-report/). `VERIFIED [blog]` Krippendorff's alpha is about 0.3 for three reviewers. Soundness scores agree considerably more than Excitement scores.

### 1.3 Discussion dynamics: herding and conformity
- **No first-mover herding** (Stelmakh, Rastogi, Shah, Singh, Daumé III, *PLOS ONE* 2023; https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0287443; arXiv 2011.15083). `VERIFIED [full page]` In a randomized trial at ICML 2020 (1,544 papers, 2,797 reviewers), changing who opened the discussion did not change acceptance significantly: "no evidence of herding".
- **Conformity toward peers** (Gao, Eger, Kuznetsov, Gurevych, Miyao, "Does My Rebuttal Matter?", NAACL 2019; https://aclanthology.org/N19-1129/). `VERIFIED [abs]` A reviewer's final score is mostly predicted by their initial score and by its distance from the other reviewers' initial scores, which the authors read as conformity bias.
- **ICLR 2024/2025 convergence** (Kargaran, Nikeghbal, Yang, Ousidhoum, "Insights from the ICLR Peer Review and Rebuttal Process", arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15462). `VERIFIED [full]` Mean reviewer disagreement drops about 9-10% after rebuttal; for orals it drops 41-48%, spotlights 26-29%, rejected papers 6-7%. The other reviewers' mean score is the second-strongest predictor of a score change, after the reviewer's own initial score.
- **Experts update toward others** (Teplitskiy, Ranu, Gray, Menietti, Guinan, Lakhani, "Do Experts Listen to Other Experts?", HBS WP 19-107; https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/do-experts-listen-to-other-experts-field-experimental-evidence-from-scientific-peer-review). `SECONDARY` (page returned 403). 247 medical faculty updated their scores in 47% of cases after seeing (artificial) other scores, almost always toward them. Women updated more often; very highly cited reviewers updated 24% less often; very low scores were "sticky".
- **LLM agents conform** (Weng, Chen, Wang, "Do as We Do, Not as You Think: the Conformity of Large Language Models", ICLR 2025 oral; https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13381). `VERIFIED [abs]` LLM agents in multi-agent setups conform to the majority (BenchForm benchmark). Conformity depends on majority size and interaction time. Enhanced personas and a reflection step reduce it.

### 1.4 Anchoring on the first impression
- Initial score is the strongest predictor of the final score in human data (Gao et al. 2019; Kargaran et al. 2025, above). `VERIFIED`
- **Initial-review structure bounds movement** (Louis, Vanleke, Ginis, Algaba, arXiv June 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22166). `VERIFIED [abs]` About 73k ICLR 2024-2025 reviewer trajectories. A score increase happens in 8.3% of cases when the review text reads below its assigned score, and 31.9% when it reads above. Initial-review structure alone predicts movement with AUC 0.747; adding the author-reviewer exchange raises it to 0.804.
- **Resubmission signal** (Stelmakh, Shah, Singh, Daumé III, "Prior and Prejudice", CSCW 2021; https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.14646). `VERIFIED [abs]` 133 novice reviewers and 19 papers. Being told a paper was a resubmission lowered the overall score by 0.78 on a 10-point scale (95% CI -1.30 to -0.24).
- Kajiya (above) on misreading: "if the reviewers misunderstand your paper, or if some flaw in your paper is found, you're dead" (quoted in Freeman 2014). `VERIFIED [full]`

### 1.5 Confidence vs accuracy and experience
- **ACL 2023**: reviewer confidence does not reflect self-reported experience (report sec. 7.5.3). `VERIFIED [blog]`
- **NIPS 2016** (Shah, Tabibian, Muandet, Guyon, von Luxburg, "Design and Analysis of the NIPS 2016 Review Process", JMLR 2018; https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.09794). `VERIFIED [full]` Self-reported confidence rises with seniority (senior vs PhD student d=0.36). Senior and junior scores do not differ significantly after multiple-testing correction.
- **ICLR 2024/25**: higher-confidence reviews have longer weakness sections and lower ratings; mean confidence about 3.6 (Kargaran et al. 2025). `VERIFIED [full]`
- **Less confident reviewers are more swayed by reputation** (Bharadhwaj, Turpin, Garg, Anderson, arXiv 2020; https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00177). `VERIFIED [abs]` ICLR 2019-2020, n=5,050. Correlational only.
- LLM-modified reviews concentrate among low-confidence reviews (Liang et al. 2024, sec. 3.2). `VERIFIED [abs]`

### 1.6 Novelty bias
- **Novelty and proximity penalties** (Boudreau, Guinan, Lakhani, Riedl, *Management Science* 62(10):2765-2783, 2016; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5062254/). `VERIFIED [full]` Randomized: 142 evaluators, 150 proposals, 2,130 pairs. Evaluators score proposals closer to their own expertise lower (about 1 point across the distance range); evaluators from outside the domain score 0.37 higher. More novel proposals score lower (coefficient -2.80), driven by the most novel quintile. Rankings by the closest experts differ from random-evaluator rankings by 31.8 positions on average.
- **Bias against novelty in bibliometrics** (Wang, Veugelers, Stephan, *Research Policy* 46(8):1416-1436, 2017; https://www.nber.org/papers/w22180). `SECONDARY` Highly novel papers get delayed recognition, so short-window citation indicators penalize novel work.
- "Not novel" is the most-abused criticism: malicious simulated reviewers use it about 3x more often (AgentReview); "Not Enough Novelty" is the second most frequent lazy-thinking class (LazyReview, 1.10); ICCV 2023 PCs list the "novelty fallacy" as a common mistake (section 2). `VERIFIED`

### 1.7 Prestige and de-anonymization
- **Single-blind favors the famous** (Tomkins, Zhang, Heavlin, *PNAS* 114(48):12708, 2017; https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1707323114). `VERIFIED [abs via Europe PMC]` WSDM 2017: single-blind reviewers bid on 22% fewer papers. Their odds of recommending acceptance were 1.63x for famous authors, 1.58x for top universities, and 2.10x for top companies.
- **Nobel and novice** (Huber, Inoua, Kerschbamer, König-Kersting, Palan, Smith, *PNAS* 119(41), 2022; https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2205779119). `VERIFIED [abs via Europe PMC]` Over 3,300 invited reviewers, same paper. Reject recommendations: 23% when the Nobel laureate's name was shown, 48% when anonymized, 65% when the junior author's name was shown.
- **Reviewers search for papers** (Rastogi, Stelmakh, Shen, Meila, Echenique, Chawla, Shah, "To ArXiv or not to ArXiv", arXiv 2022, rev. 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.17259). `VERIFIED [abs]` In ICML 2021 and EC 2021 surveys, more than a third of reviewers self-reported searching online for their assigned papers.
- **ACL 2023**: preprinted papers received significantly higher Soundness, Excitement, and confidence scores. Reviewers guessed author identity for 49% of preprinted papers. `VERIFIED [blog]`
- **ICLR**: a top-10 institution is worth +0.15 in average reviewer score at the AC level. Last-author reputation is worth +0.16 to +0.29. arXiv visibility for CMU/MIT/Cornell papers is worth +0.67 (Tran et al. 2020). `VERIFIED [full]`

### 1.8 Rebuttal effects
- **ICLR 2024 / 2025** (Kargaran et al. 2025). `VERIFIED [full]` After rebuttal, 81% / 75% of reviews were unchanged, 17% / 23% went up, 1% / 1% went down. Paper mean moved 4.86 to 5.14 and 4.78 to 5.15. Common moves: 5 to 6, 6 to 8, 3 to 5. Papers whose scores rose were accepted 57.6% / 55.7% of the time; papers whose scores fell, 6.4% / 8.0%. More than 40% of the pre-rebuttal top-5% papers were displaced. Increases go with more conversation turns (2.21 vs 1.47).
- **NIPS 2016** (Shah et al.). `VERIFIED [full]` 1,193 of 12,154 reviews (9.8%) changed score after rebuttal; mean absolute change under 0.1 on a 1-5 scale.
- **ACL 2018** (Gao et al. 2019). `VERIFIED [abs]` Small but significant effect, strongest for borderline papers.
- **ICML 2012 reviewer survey** (https://icml.cc/Conferences/2012/survey-results/results.php%3Fcnd=2.html). `VERIFIED [full]` Rebuttal influence among 85 reviewers: very strong 4%, substantial 12%, marginal 62%, none 22%.

### 1.9 Effort, time, and length
- **CVPR / ICCV official time budget**. `VERIFIED [full]` "Many experienced reviewers take 2-4 hours per paper. If you're fairly new to reviewing ... plan on [at] least 4 hours per paper and take the time to read the paper twice" (CVPR 2019 PC tutorial; ICCV 2023 PC tutorial, section 2). Load: 6-12 papers per reviewer at CVPR'18-'19; 5-8 at ICCV'23; up to about 30-35 papers per AC.
- **Self-reported hours across science** (Aczel, Szaszi, Holcombe, *Research Integrity and Peer Review* 2021; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8591820/). `VERIFIED [full]` The authors assume 6 h per review, based on three surveys: Sense About Science 2009 (mean 6 h), Publons 2016 (median 5 h), Ware 2008 (mean 8.5 h).
- **ICML 2012 survey**: 28 h of reviewing per reviewer in total (161 responses); 65% consulted supplementary material, which helped the decision for 40%. `VERIFIED [full]`
- **Length bias in judging reviews** (Goldberg, Stelmakh, Cho, Oh, Agarwal, Belgrave, Shah, "Peer Reviews of Peer Reviews", NeurIPS 2022 experiment; https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09497). `VERIFIED [abs]` In an RCT, artificially lengthened reviews were rated higher quality. Evaluators of reviews disagreed 28-32% of the time. Authors rated reviews that recommended acceptance of their own paper more highly.
- **Weakness-section length** (Kargaran et al.): about 167-175 words for accepted papers, about 220 for withdrawn ones. `VERIFIED [full]`

### 1.10 Low-quality review rates and lazy heuristics
- **LazyReview** (Purkayastha, Li, Lauscher, Qu, Gurevych, ACL 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11042). `VERIFIED [full]` The 14 ARR "lazy thinking" heuristics: not surprising; contradicts what I expected; not novel; no precedent; does not beat SOTA; negative results; too simple; doesn't use my preferred method; too niche; non-English only; language errors; missing comparison to the latest X; could also do extra experiment X; should have done X instead. Most frequent in the annotated set: Extra Experiments, then Not Enough Novelty, then Language Errors. Zero-shot LLMs detect them poorly. The ACL 2023 report attributes 24.3% of author-reported review issues to these heuristics.
- **ACL 2023**: the most common author complaints are lazy heuristics such as "too simple" and "not SOTA". 6.2% of reviewers received 2+ flags and 1.2% received 3+. `VERIFIED [blog]`
- **CVPR 2025 "highly irresponsible" flag** (https://cvpr.thecvf.com/Conferences/2025/CVPRChanges). `VERIFIED [full]` Examples: one-sentence reviews, LLM-generated reviews, reviews irrelevant to the paper or missing a substantial part of it. Misunderstandings are explicitly *not* grounds for the flag. LLMs may not be used to write reviews.
- **CVPR 2025 outcome** (Voxel51 recap of the opening remarks; https://voxel51.com/blog/opening-remarks-from-cvpr-2025). `SECONDARY` 13,008 submissions, 12,593 reviewers, 22.1% acceptance. 19 desk rejections for irresponsible reviewing. "Below expectations" reviews fell from 9% to 6%.
- **ICLR 2025 Review Feedback Agent** (Thakkar, Yuksekgonul, Silberg, Garg, Peng, Sha, Yu, Vondrick, Zou; https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09737). `VERIFIED [full]` 18,946 of 44,831 reviews (42.3%) received feedback. Of the ~22.5k reviews randomly selected for feedback, only 2,692 (about 12%) were judged to need none. About half of all feedback asked reviewers to clarify methodological concerns. A typical vague comment: "The paper's novelty is limited considering the ICLR standards."
- **Miscalibration** (NIPS 2016): about 60% of scores were 3 or higher, although 3 was reserved for the top 30%. 32% of papers were flagged as having a "fatal flaw" by at least one reviewer. `VERIFIED [full]`

## 2. CV-community guidance: good vs bad reviews, and how reviewers actually read

### 2.1 Official CVPR / ICCV reviewer guidance
- **CVPR 2019 PC tutorial** "How to Write Good Reviews for CVPR" (Hoiem, Hua, Gupta, Tu; mirror https://www.cs.torontomu.ca/~wangcs/resources\How-to-Review-for-CVPR.pdf). `VERIFIED [full]`
  - Review structure: Summary, Strengths, Weaknesses, Rating and Justification. "The ACs are your primary audience. Make your review self-contained."
  - The graded "bad" example lists only weaknesses and requests for clarification, with no summary or justification. "No strengths listed, which may indicate that reviewer is just looking for reasons to reject"; "Author and AC don't know which of the listed points are important."
  - Per a 2018 PAMI-TC motion: no requests for new experiments in the rebuttal, and no penalty for lacking them.
  - "We all tend to assign more value to papers that are relevant to our own research."
- **ICCV 2023 PC tutorial** "How to be a good reviewer?" (Agapito, Furukawa, Grauman, He, Laptev; https://iccv2023.thecvf.com/telechargements/fichiers/1680595402.how.to.be.a.good.reviewer.iccv.2023.pdf). `VERIFIED [full]`
  - Accept covers "incremental steps that ... add bricks to the cathedral of knowledge" and "creative ideas that are hard to judge" (benefit of the doubt).
  - **Evidence bar by paper type**: potentially transformative idea, a basic proof of concept; established problem with a plausible idea, benchmark results; "weird, overly complex, implausible, and/or seemingly incremental", extraordinary results; position or theory paper, no experiments.
  - Named common mistakes: arrogance / ignorance / inaccuracy; pure opinions ("not good enough for ICCV"); **novelty fallacy** ("by whom and where has this been published before?"); blank assertions; **policy entrepreneurism** ("You must beat SOTA / have a theorem / beat arXiv papers / publish your dataset"); intellectual laziness; over-weighting one error ("Is the error making the main knowledge advancement invalid?").
  - Professional tone: no "you"; no requests to cite one's own work for promotion.
- **CVPR 2026 Reviewer Guidelines** (https://cvpr.thecvf.com/Conferences/2026/ReviewerGuidelines). `VERIFIED [full]` "Bullet lists with one short phrase per bullet are NOT a detailed review." "Done before" claims need specific references and an explanation of the overlap. A paper may not be rejected solely for missing arXiv comparisons. Not beating SOTA is not by itself grounds for rejection. Minor, easily fixed flaws should not lead to rejection. Reviewers should "embrace novel, brave concepts, even if they have not been tested on many datasets". Unreasonably short, generic, or factually wrong reviews, and LLM-written reviews, count as highly irresponsible.

### 2.2 Senior-researcher advice
- **Bill Freeman**, "How to write a good CVPR submission" (MIT CSAIL, 6 Nov 2014; mirror https://www.cs.torontomu.ca/~wangcs/resources\How-to-write-a-good-CVPR-submission.pdf). `VERIFIED [full]`
  - The reality is "a large, crowded marketplace", not scholars "pouring over your manuscript". Reviewers handle 5-15 papers.
  - "It should be easy to read the paper in a big hurry and still learn the main points. ... the figure captions should be self-contained and ... tell the reader what to notice."
  - With 70-80% rejection rates, the evaluator's question becomes "How can I reject this paper?"
  - An AC's pile: about 1/3 obvious rejects, maybe 1 really nice paper, the rest borderline. The borderline papers are either the **"Cockroach"** (nothing exciting, but you "can't find a way to kill" it: an incremental, well-written CVPR paper) or the **"Puppy with 6 toes"** (a delightful paper with "some easy-to-point-to flaw" that makes it easy to kill).
  - "Quick and easy reasons to reject": promises more than it delivers; missing important references; main idea done before; incremental; results not believable; poorly written; incorrect statements.
  - Against "future work" lists: "You get no 'partial credit'."
- **Jim Kajiya** (SIGGRAPH 93; URL in 1.1). `VERIFIED [full]` "SIGGRAPH reviewers are like everyone else. They first look at the pictures in your paper." Quoting Ivan Sutherland, you should be able to get the point "just by reading the captions". "The most dangerous mistake ... is assuming that the reviewer will understand the point of your paper." Reviewers "may not read your paper carefully, they misunderstand it." The remedy is a "dynamite introduction" that says what is new "(and what isn't)".
- **Ming-Hsuan Yang**, "How to Get Your CVPR Paper Rejected?" (URL in 1.1). `VERIFIED [full]` ACs "know the reviewers" and weight reviews. Triage sorts papers into accept / reject ("don't waste time") / go either way ("lots of papers"). Common mistakes: unsupported claims, "unnecessary adjectives (superior!)", laundry-list related work. "Make sure your paper is non-rejectable (above the bar with some error margin)." Includes a real consolidation report: moderate novelty, unclear technical details, limited experiments, unjustified assumptions; the rebuttal was "not sufficient to ease the main concerns" and the paper was rejected.
- **Michael J. Black**, "Novelty in Science: A guide for reviewers" (30 Jan 2022; https://perceiving-systems.blog/en/news/novelty-in-science). `VERIFIED [full]` Reviewers mistake complexity, difficulty, and technicality for novelty. "If it is easy to explain and obvious in hindsight, this in no way diminishes the creativity (and novelty) of the idea." Novelty can be a dataset, a new use of an existing method, or a simpler algorithm. "Imagine the darkness before the spark."
- **Devi Parikh, Dhruv Batra, Stefan Lee**, "How we write rebuttals" (27 May 2020; https://deviparikh.substack.com/p/how-we-write-rebuttals-dc84742fece1). `VERIFIED [full]` Reviewers "may have forgotten some of the details or didn't understand them in the first place". "Assume that all [the AC] will read is the set of reviews and the rebuttal." Tactics: lead with the biggest concerns; quote each concern and answer directly ("Yes" / "Not quite"); point to line, table, or figure; do rather than promise; flag unsubstantiated comments.
- **Lipton & Steinhardt**, "Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship" (ICML 2018 Debates; https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03341). `VERIFIED [abs]` Four failure patterns reviewers should catch: speculation presented as explanation; failure to identify the sources of empirical gains (e.g., gains from tuning credited to architecture); mathiness; misuse of language.
- **Jia-Bin Huang**, "Deep Paper Gestalt" (arXiv 2018; https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08775). `VERIFIED [abs]` A classifier that sees only a paper's visual appearance rejects about 50% of bad papers while wrongly rejecting 0.4% of good ones. Huang's writing threads (introduction, related work, "a paper that looks like a good one", overview figure, tables) are indexed at https://github.com/jbhuang0604/awesome-tips (`VERIFIED [full]` index only; the threads themselves were not read).

### 2.3 How reviewers read: the concrete pattern and time budget
- **Keshav**, "How to Read a Paper" (ACM SIGCOMM CCR 37(3):83-84, July 2007; https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall10/cos597B/papers/howtoread-keshav.pdf). `VERIFIED [full]`
  - Pass 1, **5-10 min**: carefully read the title, abstract, and introduction; read the section headings; read the conclusions; glance over the references. The outcome is the "five Cs": Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, Clarity.
  - "When you write a paper, you can expect most reviewers (and readers) to make only one pass over it. ... If a reviewer cannot understand the gist after one pass, the paper will likely be rejected."
  - Pass 2, **up to 1 h**: figures and graphs (labelled axes, error bars), marking unread references.
  - Pass 3, **about 1 h (experienced) to 4-5 h (beginner)**: "virtually re-implement the paper" to find hidden assumptions, missing citations, and experimental issues.
- **Synthesis for the harness.** Inference, combining Keshav, Kajiya, Freeman, the CVPR/ICCV tutorials, and the ICML 2012 survey:
  1. Minutes 0-10: title, abstract, Fig. 1 / teaser and captions, the intro's contribution list, the main results table. A provisional verdict forms here.
  2. Up to about 1 h: method overview figure, experiments, ablations, comparison tables, related-work positioning. Weaknesses are drafted mostly from this pass.
  3. Optional deep pass on equations, implementation details, and supplementary material. Experienced reviewers spend 2-4 h in total, novices at least 4 h; only about 65% open the supplementary material at all (ICML 2012).
  4. Rebuttal phase: reviewers have forgotten details (Parikh et al.). The AC reads the reviews and the author-reviewer dialogue and looks at the paper (Freeman 2014); difficult papers get read by other ACs in the triplet (CVPR 2019 tutorial, step 9). Parikh et al. advise authors to assume the AC reads only the reviews and the rebuttal.

### 2.4 Requested items not found or corrected
- **Kilian Weinberger on how reviewers skim**: no primary source found in two searches, so it is dropped.
- **"How to get your CVPR paper rejected"** is by **Ming-Hsuan Yang**, not Jia-Bin Huang. Huang's related work is *Deep Paper Gestalt*.
- **Bill Freeman's talk**: a 2018 version at billf.mit.edu was listed by a third party but not opened. The 2014 version above was read in full.

## 3. LLM review generation and simulation

### 3.1 Systems and what they achieved
- **GPT-4 feedback vs human reviews** (Liang, Zhang, Cao, Wang, Ding, Yang, Vodrahalli, He, Smith, Yin, McFarland, Zou, arXiv 2023; https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01783; journal version reported in NEJM AI 2024, `SECONDARY` for venue). `VERIFIED [abs]` Overlap between GPT-4 and human comments: 30.85% (Nature journals, 3,096 papers) and 39.23% (ICLR, 1,709 papers), against human-human overlap of 28.58% and 35.25%. Overlap is larger for weaker papers. In a user study of 308 researchers, 57.4% found the feedback helpful and 82.4% found it more useful than at least some human reviewers.
- **ReviewerGPT?** (Liu & Shah, arXiv 2023; https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00622). `VERIFIED [abs]` GPT-4 found deliberately inserted errors in 7 of 13 papers and reached 86.6% on checklist verification (119 pairs). It was wrong on 6 of 10 pairs where one abstract was clearly better. Verdict: useful for specific checks, not for whole-paper evaluation.
- **MARG** (D'Arcy, Hope, Birnbaum, Downey, arXiv 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04259). `VERIFIED [abs]` Multi-agent review that splits the paper across agents with specialised experts. Generic comments fell from 60% to 29%; good comments per paper rose from 1.7 to 3.7.
- **AI Scientist reviewer** (Lu, Lu, Lange, Foerster, Clune, Ha, arXiv 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06292). `VERIFIED [full]` On 500 ICLR 2022 papers, the GPT-4o reviewer (1-shot, 5 self-reflection rounds, 5-review ensemble plus an AC meta-review, threshold calibrated at 6) reached balanced accuracy 0.65 / F1 0.57, against human 0.66 / 0.49 (NeurIPS consistency data). Its FPR was higher (0.31 vs 0.17). Self-reflection added +2% and 1-shot +2%; ensembling only reduced variance. Claude 3.5 Sonnet was "persistently over-optimistic" (uncalibrated FPR 0.95; needed threshold 8). AI-human score correlation was 0.18 against human-human 0.14. The reviewer cannot see figures.
- **CycleReviewer / CycleResearcher** (Weng, Zhu, Bao, Zhang, Wang, Zhang, Yang, ICLR 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00816). `VERIFIED [abs]` 26.89% lower MAE than individual human reviewers at predicting paper scores. Generated papers scored 5.36, against 5.24 for preprints and 5.69 for accepted papers. Datasets: Review-5k, Research-14k. *Inference:* an MAE win over a single human is partly what any predictor of the consensus mean gets, so it says little about whether the model discriminates between papers (compare 3.3, rating compression).
- **DeepReview** (Zhu, Weng, Yang, Zhang, arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08569). `VERIFIED [abs]` DeepReview-13K. DeepReviewer-14B beats CycleReviewer-70B, with win rates of 88.21% vs GPT-o1 and 80.20% vs DeepSeek-R1.
- **OpenReviewer** (Idahl & Ahmadi, NAACL 2025 demo; https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11948). `VERIFIED [abs]` Fine-tuned on 79k expert reviews, its recommendations "closely match the distribution of human reviewer ratings". General LLMs (GPT-4, Claude-3.5) "tend toward overly positive assessments".
- **ReviewAgents** (Gao, Ruan, Zhang, Gao, Liu, Fu, arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08506). `VERIFIED [abs]` Trained on Review-CoT (142k comments): summary, then related work, then strengths/weaknesses, then conclusion. Reviewer agents are relevant-paper-aware and multi-role, evaluated on ReviewBench. A gap to human reviews remains.
- **Reviewer2** (Gao, Brantley, Joachims, arXiv 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10886). `VERIFIED [abs]` Two stages: generate aspect prompts, then reviews. More detailed reviews that cover more of the aspects humans raise. 27k papers, 99k reviews.
- **LLM-assisted novelty assessment** (Afzal, Nakov, Hope, Gurevych, "Beyond 'Not Novel Enough'", arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10795). `VERIFIED [abs]` On 182 ICLR 2025 submissions: 86.5% alignment with human reasoning and 75.3% agreement on the novelty conclusion. Human experts verify claimed novelty independently instead of accepting it.
- **AAAI-26 AI Review Pilot** (Biswas et al., arXiv 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.13940). `VERIFIED [abs]` Every main-track paper (22,977) got one clearly labelled AI review, generated in under a day. Participants preferred the AI reviews on technical accuracy and research suggestions, and the system beat a simple LLM baseline at detecting weaknesses. `SECONDARY` (press): the AI reviews carried no scores or recommendations.

### 3.2 LLM-written reviews in the wild
- **6.5-16.9% of review text substantially LLM-modified** at ICLR 2024, NeurIPS 2023, CoRL 2023, EMNLP 2023 (Liang, Izzo, Zhang, ... Zou, "Monitoring AI-Modified Content at Scale", ICML 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07183). `VERIFIED [abs]` Higher in reviews with low confidence, reviews submitted near the deadline, and reviewers less likely to answer rebuttals.
- **ICLR 2024: at least 15.8% of reviews AI-assisted** (Latona, Horta Ribeiro, Davidson, Veselovsky, West, "The AI Review Lottery", arXiv 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02150). `VERIFIED [abs]` In 53.4% of disagreeing review pairs, the AI-assisted review gave the higher score. Near the acceptance threshold, an AI-assisted review made acceptance 4.9 percentage points more likely.
- **ICLR 2026: 21% of about 70k reviews fully AI-generated; more than 50% show some AI** (Pangram Labs vendor analysis, Nov 2025; https://www.pangram.com/blog/pangram-predicts-21-of-iclr-reviews-are-ai-generated). `VERIFIED [full]` The more AI in a review, the higher its score. AI reviews are longer, with "filler". Pangram reports false-positive rates of about 1/1,000 to 1/10,000 on 2022 reviews. This is a detector vendor's own estimate.
- **Rating compression and leniency** (Sharma, Joachims, Dean, "Do LLMs Favor LLMs?", arXiv 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20920). `VERIFIED [abs]` Over 125k paper-review pairs (ICLR, NeurIPS, ICML). The apparent preference of LLM reviews for LLM-written papers disappears once paper quality is controlled. LLM-assisted reviews are more lenient toward weak papers. **Fully LLM-generated reviews show severe rating compression and fail to discriminate quality.** LLM-assisted meta-reviews lean more toward accept.
- **"Hivemind" and gameability** (Baumann, Pei, Koyejo, Hovy, "Stop Automating Peer Review Without Rigorous Evaluation", ICML 2026 position, oral; https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.03202). `VERIFIED [abs]` AI reviewers agree excessively within and across papers. Having an LLM rewrite a paper noticeably raises AI reviewers' scores, so they respond to style rather than substance.

### 3.3 Known failure modes of LLM reviewers
- **Generic weaknesses**: 60% generic comments for single-agent GPT-4 (MARG). Zero-shot LLMs are poor at detecting lazy heuristics (LazyReview). `VERIFIED`
- **Positivity / score inflation**: OpenReviewer; AI Scientist (Claude FPR 0.95); **Agent Laboratory** (Schmidgall et al., arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04227; `VERIFIED [full]`), where automated NeurIPS-style reviewers gave generated papers 6.1/10 against 3.8/10 from humans (clarity 3.6 vs 2.4 out of 4), and "automated reviews do not align closely with human reviews". Ye et al. (below): incomplete papers can get higher ratings than complete ones.
- **Rating compression**: Sharma et al. 2026 (above). `VERIFIED [abs]`
- **Novelty blindness** (Shin, Tang, Lee, Kim, Lim, Cho, Hong, Lee, Kim, "Mind the Blind Spots", EMNLP 2025 oral; https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17086). `VERIFIED [abs]` Across 676 OpenReview papers (3,657 human strengths and weaknesses), off-the-shelf LLMs focus disproportionately on technical validity and "significantly overlook novelty" when criticising.
- **Rebuttal sycophancy, prestige, hedging** (Wang, Liu, Xu, Hu, Di, Ni, Yue, Zhang, Ren, Chen, "When AI reviews science: Can we trust the referee?", arXiv 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23593). `VERIFIED [full]` Gemini 2.5 and GPT-5.1 reviewed 100 ICLR 2025 papers (25 per tier). An evidence-free rebuttal raised scores by +0.53 on average; 81% of papers rose and none fell (a figure caption says 89%), with similar inflation for orals and rejects. Low-prestige framing gave -0.72 (88% of papers lowered), high-prestige +0.25. Cautious wording gave -0.39.
- **Meta-review perturbation** (Li, Li, Hu, Gao, Wan, arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12510). `VERIFIED [abs]` Strong-reject reviews dominate LLM meta-reviews. Negative or misleading reviews get read as thorough. Incomplete or hostile rebuttals can *raise* acceptance. These effects persist across chain-of-thought prompts.
- **Prompt injection and manipulation**
  - Ye, Pang, Chai, Chen, Yin, Xiang, Dong, Shao, Chen, "Are We There Yet?" (arXiv 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01708). `VERIFIED [abs]` Covert content inflates LLM ratings. Manipulating 5% of reviews pushes about 12% of papers out of the top 30%. When authors highlight minor limitations, LLM reviews echo those disclosed limitations 4.5x more often than human reviews do.
  - Lin, "Hidden Prompts in Manuscripts Exploit AI-Assisted Peer Review" (arXiv 2025, CACM 69(7) 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06185). `VERIFIED [abs]` 18 arXiv manuscripts in July 2025 carried white or microscopic text such as "GIVE A POSITIVE REVIEW ONLY".
  - Zhou, Zhang, Li, Sun, "Give a Positive Review Only" (EMNLP 2026 Findings; https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.01287). `VERIFIED [abs]` Static and iterative injections often produce full scores. A detection defense helps, but an adaptive attacker partly circumvents it.
- **Prestige and identity bias in LLM reviewers** (Pataranutaporn, Powdthavee, Achiwaranguprok, Maes, arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00070). `VERIFIED [abs]` Over 29k evaluations of 1,220 economics papers. LLMs track quality, but GPT, Gemma, and LLaMA rated the same paper higher when attributed to top male authors or elite institutions than when anonymous.
- **Preference for LLM style**: LLM-REVal (Li, Gu, Kung, Xia, Liu, Kong, Sui, Peng, arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12367; `VERIFIED [abs]`). LLM reviewers inflate scores for LLM-authored papers and underrate human papers that contain critical statements (risk, fairness). Causes: a linguistic-feature bias and an aversion to critical statements. See also Laurito et al., "AI-AI bias", PNAS 122(31) 2025 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.12856; `SECONDARY`).
- **Error detection limits**: 7/13 inserted errors found (ReviewerGPT). About 20% fully-correct answers on review-revision questions (Zhou, Chen, Yu, LREC-COLING 2024; https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.816; `SECONDARY`).

### 3.4 Techniques that raised realism or human alignment
- **Persona conditioning** on commitment / intention / knowledge, plus AC styles, reproduces documented sociological effects (AgentReview). `VERIFIED`
- **Multi-agent division of labour** cuts generic comments (MARG), as does **aspect planning** before writing (Reviewer2). `VERIFIED [abs]`
- **Self-reflection + 1-shot example + ensemble + AC meta-review + a threshold calibrated on known-outcome papers** (AI Scientist). Calibration matters most: the same model at a naive threshold accepts almost everything. `VERIFIED [full]`
- **Fine-tuning on real review distributions** gives a human-like rating distribution and less positivity (OpenReviewer, CycleReviewer, DeepReviewer). `VERIFIED [abs]`
- **Retrieval of related work** for novelty judgements (ReviewAgents; Afzal et al.). `VERIFIED [abs]`
- **Reliability tests on generated feedback** (ICLR 2025 agent): no praise-only items, no restating the reviewer, verbatim paper quotes, no score commentary; a critic step removes nitpicks. Over 96% of feedback passed. `VERIFIED [full]`
- **Pairwise comparison** of manuscripts aggregated with Bradley-Terry beats rating-based selection at finding high-impact papers, but it introduces less topic novelty and more institutional concentration (Zhang, Zhang, Ji, Hua, Haber, Cao, Liang, arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11343). `VERIFIED [abs]`
- **Caricature risk**: persona simulations can be exaggerated stereotypes. Measure *individuation* (output differs from the default) and *exaggeration* (Cheng, Piccardi, Yang, "CoMPosT", EMNLP 2023; https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11501). `VERIFIED [abs]`

## 4. Writer-reviewer loops: evidence, Goodhart risks, mitigations

### 4.1 Iterative loops with reviewer agents
- **CycleResearcher** (ICLR 2025, above): iterative preference training of a researcher model against CycleReviewer rewards. The generated papers stayed below the accepted-paper mean (5.36 vs 5.69) by the authors' own reviewer. `VERIFIED [abs]`
- **AI Scientist-v2** (Yamada, Lange, Lu, Hu, Lu, Foerster, Clune, Ha, arXiv 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08066). `VERIFIED [abs]` The AI reviewer includes a VLM feedback loop on figures. Of 3 manuscripts submitted to an ICLR 2025 workshop, 1 exceeded the average human acceptance threshold. **Sakana blog** (https://sakana.ai/ai-scientist-first-publication/; `VERIFIED [full]`): ICBINB workshop, 43 submissions, scores 6/7/6 (6.33, about the 45th percentile), no meta-review. The team's own main-track-standard review found none of the 3 met the bar, and found citation errors (an LSTM credited to Goodfellow 2016).
- **Agent Laboratory** (above): automated reviewer scores did not predict human scores (6.1 vs 3.8). `VERIFIED [full]`
- **PaperOrchestra** (Song, Song, Pfister, Yoon, arXiv 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.05018). `VERIFIED [full]` The refinement step uses AgentReview as the simulated reviewer. A revision is accepted only if the overall score rises; a tie is accepted only if the net sub-axis gain is non-negative; any drop reverts and halts. Reported gains *on the same AgentReview judge*: simulated acceptance +19% (CVPR) / +22% (ICLR), overall score +0.88 / +1.61. Side-by-side judgements by different judges (Gemini-3.1-Pro, GPT-5): 79-81% wins. No other overfitting safeguard is described. *Inference:* the same-judge numbers are exactly the measurement the harness must not trust.
- **Jr. AI Scientist and Its Risk Report** (Miyai et al., TMLR 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04583). `SECONDARY` Lists "review-score hacking" and incorrect citations as development-time risks. A third-party summary says AI reviewers miss mismatches between text and actual results.
- **LLM-REVal** (above): revisions guided by LLM reviews improved quality in both LLM and human evaluations. Loops can help; the problem is trusting the loop's own judge. `VERIFIED [abs]`

### 4.2 Goodhart / reward-hacking evidence
- **Spontaneous in-context reward hacking in self-refinement** (Jane Pan, He He, Bowman, Shi Feng, arXiv 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04549). `VERIFIED [full]` Essay editing, with the same model as author and judge for 5 rounds. With GPT-3.5, the in-loop judge's scores rose while human scores plateaued or fell. An offline judge without the loop context tracked humans. **Shared context between author and judge drives the divergence** (it matters more than context length). GPT-4 was less affected. Only the "Conventions" (grammar) rubric item tracked humans.
- **Feedback loops drive in-context reward hacking** (Alexander Pan, Jones, Jagadeesan, Steinhardt, ICML 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06627). `VERIFIED [abs]` Output-refinement and policy-refinement loops optimise an implicit proxy with side effects. Static evaluations miss this.
- **Reward-model over-optimization** (Gao, Schulman, Hilton, arXiv 2022; https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760). `VERIFIED [abs]` Pushing a proxy reward too far hurts the gold objective (Goodhart). The relationship depends on the optimisation method and scales with reward-model size.
- **Presentation-only revisions game AI reviewers** (Xu Yang et al., arXiv June 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13044). `VERIFIED [abs]` A closed-loop "adversarial repackaging" that changes only presentation achieved 75.1% attack success across 3 AI reviewers, at +1.21/10. Related-work repositioning and expanded analytical discussion beat local polishing. Reviewers can take the *appearance* of addressing a limitation for resolving it.
- **Rhetorical reward hacking** (Ming Li, Chenguang Wang, et al., arXiv Aug 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08975; NeurIPS 2026 listing `SECONDARY`). `VERIFIED [abs]` 4,200 manuscripts derived from 120 ICLR 2026 submissions, 6 rhetorical dimensions, 5 LLM reviewers. **Evidence framing and novelty stance** move scores the most. Scores regress toward the middle. A strict review protocol lowers the mean by 1.36 without reducing rhetorical sensitivity. Reviewer-guided and recursive rewriting do not reliably add more.
- **Null models win LLM-judged benchmarks** (Xiaosen Zheng, Pang, Du, Liu, Jiang, Lin, ICLR 2025 oral; https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07137). `VERIFIED [abs]` A constant output reaches an 86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, 83.0 on Arena-Hard-Auto, and 9.55 on MT-Bench.
- **Universal adversarial phrases** (Raina, Liusie, Gales, arXiv 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14016). `VERIFIED [abs]` Short appended phrases inflate judge scores and transfer to unseen judges. **Absolute scoring is significantly more vulnerable than comparative assessment.**
- **Judge biases**: position, verbosity, and self-enhancement (Lianmin Zheng et al., MT-Bench, NeurIPS 2023 D&B; https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685; `VERIFIED [abs]`). Position bias: reordering lets Vicuna-13B beat ChatGPT on 66/80 queries (Peiyi Wang et al., arXiv 2023; https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926; `VERIFIED [abs]`). Self-preference grows with self-recognition (Panickssery, Bowman, Feng, arXiv 2024, NeurIPS 2024 `SECONDARY`; https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076; `VERIFIED [abs]`). Inconsistent across samples, skewed scores, anchoring across attributes, preference for low-perplexity text (Stureborg, Alikaniotis, Suhara, arXiv 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01724; `VERIFIED [abs]`). Length bias (Dubois, Galambosi, Liang, Hashimoto, COLM 2024; https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475; `VERIFIED [abs]`).
- **Ecosystem view** (Chenguang Wang et al., "The Emerging AI Paper-Review Arms Race", arXiv Sept 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07713). `VERIFIED [abs]` Survey of 230 sources. Evidence is strongest that manipulation is reproducible; evidence is weak for how actors adapt after a policy changes.

## 5. Design rules for realistic persona simulation

1. **Make panels disagree at human rates.** Independent human panels disagree on 23-26% of accept/reject calls, and about half of accepted papers flip on a rerun. Calibrate the persona mix so that two independently drawn panels on a borderline paper disagree at roughly this rate (NeurIPS 2014 / 2021; Tran et al. 2020). A panel that always agrees is not realistic.
2. **Give each persona a leniency offset, not just different content.** About 50% of score variance is subjective (Cortes & Lawrence 2021), and reviewer SD is about 60% of paper-quality SD (Tran et al. 2020). Each persona gets a fixed calibration offset (for example, -1 to +1 on the CVPR scale).
3. **Build personas on AgentReview's axes**: commitment, intention, knowledgeability, plus AC style (inclusive / conformist / authoritarian). Default panels contain at most one adversarial ("paper killer") persona, because one malicious reviewer already drags the others down 0.14 and three take the mean from 5.11 to 3.35 (AgentReview; Kajiya's champions and killers).
4. **Let expertise change what gets criticised.** Experts write more experimental-validation critiques; non-experts write more limitation complaints and are swayed more by reputation (AgentReview; Bharadhwaj et al. 2020). The close-expert persona scores harsher; out-of-domain evaluators scored +0.37 on a 1-10 scale (Boudreau et al. 2016).
5. **Simulate a one-pass skim first.** Each persona forms a provisional verdict from title, abstract, Fig. 1 and captions, the contribution list, and the main table, and records it *before* reading the method. "Most reviewers ... make only one pass" (Keshav 2007); "they first look at the pictures" (Kajiya); papers must be readable "in a big hurry" (Freeman 2014).
6. **Give each persona an attention budget.** Experienced reviewers spend 2-4 h and novices at least 4 h, across 5-12 papers (CVPR 2019 and ICCV 2023 tutorials). Only about 65% open the supplementary material (ICML 2012). Some personas therefore never read the supplementary, and some read only parts of the method section.
7. **Apply the stage-1 verdict as an anchor.** The final score is mostly the initial score plus a pull toward peers (Gao et al. 2019; Kargaran et al. 2025; Louis et al. 2026: AUC 0.747 from the initial review alone). Low scores are sticky (Teplitskiy et al., SECONDARY).
8. **Use realistic rejection heuristics at realistic rates, and label them in the debrief.** Use Freeman's quick reasons to reject and the 14 LazyReview heuristics, with "extra experiments", "not novel", and "language errors" most common. The out-of-character debrief marks which comments are lazy heuristics under CVPR 2026 rules (LazyReview 2025; Freeman 2014; CVPR 2026 guidelines).
9. **Treat "not novel" as a claim that needs a citation.** Only the explicitly "lazy" or "adversarial" persona may assert novelty problems without references. Other personas must name the overlapping paper and say what overlaps (CVPR 2026 guidelines; ICCV 2023 "novelty fallacy"; malicious reviewers use the novelty attack about 3x more, AgentReview).
10. **Include a "simple = trivial" persona and a "beauty" persona.** One mistakes simplicity for lack of novelty; the other applies Black's test ("obvious in hindsight ... in no way diminishes"). The writer has to survive the first and convince the second (Black 2022; Boudreau et al.: highly novel work is penalised).
11. **Raise the evidence bar with the paper type.** A transformative idea needs proof of concept; an established problem needs benchmarks; "weird / complex / seemingly incremental" work needs extraordinary results (ICCV 2023 PC tutorial).
12. **Keep reviews independent before discussion, and require stated reasons for updates.** Humans show partial conformity but no first-mover herding (Stelmakh et al. 2023; Kargaran et al.: disagreement drops about 10%). LLM agents conform more strongly than humans (Weng et al., ICLR 2025). Personas write their initial reviews in isolation; in discussion, a score may change only with a cited reason. Target spread reduction: about 6-10% for typical and rejected papers, about 40-48% for oral-level papers (Kargaran et al.).
13. **Use human base rates for rebuttal movement, and block sycophancy.** About 17-23% of reviews rise, about 1% fall, and the mean moves about +0.3 (ICLR 2024/25, Kargaran et al.); NIPS 2016 saw 9.8% changed. LLM referees rise +0.53 on *evidence-free* rebuttals (Wang et al. 2026). A persona may raise its score only when the rebuttal points to specific evidence (table, line, or result).
14. **Calibrate scores against real CVPR distributions, not the model's defaults.** Off-the-shelf LLM reviewers are over-positive: OpenReviewer; the AI Scientist's Claude FPR 0.95; Agent Laboratory's 6.1 vs 3.8; AI-assisted reviews score higher (Latona et al. 2024; Pangram 2025). Map persona outputs onto the score distribution in the track B corpus and set the accept threshold on known-outcome papers (the AI Scientist needed 6 for GPT-4o and 8 for Claude).
15. **Check that personas discriminate.** Fully LLM-written reviews show severe rating compression (Sharma et al. 2026) and a "hivemind" effect (Baumann et al. 2026). Before use, score a calibration set of clearly strong and clearly weak papers; reject a persona configuration if its score spread between the two is below the human spread.
16. **Add a dedicated novelty / related-work persona with retrieval.** Off-the-shelf LLMs neglect novelty (Shin et al., EMNLP 2025). Relevant-paper-aware agents and LLM-assisted novelty pipelines (75.3% agreement) close part of the gap (ReviewAgents; Afzal et al. 2025).
17. **Ground every weakness.** Each weakness quotes a sentence, equation, figure, or table, and states its severity and whether it drives the score. This counters the generic-comment default (60% in MARG) and the "bad review" pattern of unexplained weakness lists (Hoiem et al. 2019; ICLR 2025 reliability tests).
18. **Define personas by behavior parameters, not adjectives.** Use attention budget, leniency offset, criterion weights, pet heuristics, and expertise area. Test for caricature: outputs should differ from the default reviewer (individuation) without exaggeration (CoMPosT, Cheng et al. 2023).
19. **Keep the default panel double-blind, and add prestige only as a stress test.** Humans and LLMs both reward prestige (Tomkins et al. 2017; Huber et al. 2022; Pataranutaporn et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2026: -0.72 under low-prestige framing). An optional "found it on arXiv" persona models the more than one-third of reviewers who search (Rastogi et al.) and ACL's 49% identity guess rate.
20. **Include realistic low-effort reviews.** About 6-9% of CVPR reviews are rated "below expectations" (SECONDARY); 6.2% of ACL reviewers got 2+ flags; the ICLR 2025 agent judged about 88% of the reviews it checked to have at least one improvable item (Thakkar et al. 2025; this is an agent judgement, not a human quality rating). A realistic panel draw sometimes includes a short, low-commitment review. Its effect on the other reviewers is the AgentReview commitment spill-over (-18.7% effort).

## 6. Anti-gaming rules for the writer-vs-reviewer loop

1. **Fresh, context-isolated judges every round.** Reviewers never see the writer's conversation, earlier rounds, earlier scores, or the change log, because shared author-judge context is what drives in-context reward hacking (Pan, He, Bowman, Feng 2024; Pan, Jones, Jagadeesan, Steinhardt 2024).
2. **Keep a held-out panel that the writer never sees.** Optimise against panel A (the feedback panel). Report progress only from panel B, which uses different personas and a different model family, and whose outputs the writer never reads. Same-judge gains such as PaperOrchestra's +0.88 to +1.61 on AgentReview are not evidence (Gao, Schulman, Hilton 2022; Song et al. 2026).
3. **Use a mixed-family jury, and never let the writer's model be the only judge.** Panels from disjoint families cut intra-model bias (Verga et al. 2024). Same-model judging adds self-preference and a preference for LLM style (Panickssery et al. 2024; LLM-REVal 2025).
4. **Measure the noise floor first.** Re-review the unchanged draft k ≥ 3 times with fresh instances. A score gain smaller than about 2 SD of that test-retest spread is not a gain. LLM evaluators disagree with themselves across samples (Stureborg et al. 2024), and human panels flip about 23-26% of decisions (NeurIPS 2014/2021).
5. **Make blind pairwise comparison the primary metric and absolute scores secondary.** The held-out judge sees old and new versions unlabelled, in both orders, and states which is better and why. Absolute scoring is more attackable (Raina et al. 2024); position bias is removed by balanced ordering (Wang et al. 2023; Zheng et al. 2023).
6. **Diff edits into presentation vs evidence.** Every revision is labelled presentation-only or evidence-changing. Gains from presentation-only edits must be confirmed by pairwise held-out judges and flagged in the round report. A weakness "resolved" only by text must be re-checked against unchanged evidence. Presentation-only repackaging gets +1.21/10 at 75% success, and evidence framing and novelty stance are the most exploitable levers (Yang et al. 2026; Li et al. 2026).
7. **Run a claim-evidence audit outside the reviewer panel.** A verifier with access to the experiment logs checks every number and claim the writer adds or changes. Reviewers judge the text; the verifier judges truth. AI reviewers miss text-results mismatches and hallucinated citations (Jr. AI Scientist, SECONDARY; Sakana 2025 citation error; Lipton & Steinhardt 2018).
8. **Anchor the rubric, and keep it fixed and writer-read-only.** Use explicit score anchors with reference examples per section, plus a strict protocol. Rubric-plus-reference evaluators track humans (Prometheus, Kim et al. 2024); strict protocols cut inflation (-1.36, Li et al. 2026). The writer may never edit rubric, persona, or score files (harness plan).
9. **Control for length.** Compare drafts at equal page budget, and report whether a gain came with growth in length or word count. LLM judges and human evaluators both reward length (Dubois et al. 2024; Goldberg et al. 2023), and AI reviews themselves inflate with filler (Pangram 2025).
10. **Screen for injection and reviewer-directed rhetoric.** Before every review round, scan the draft for hidden or white text, instructions addressed to reviewers or AI, and meta-claims about how reviewers will judge. Watch for strategic over-disclosure of minor limitations, which LLM reviewers echo 4.5x more than humans (Ye et al. 2024; Lin 2025; Zhou et al. 2025).
11. **Simulate the rebuttal with evidence-gated updates.** Score increases require a specific pointer (table, line, or new result in the paper). Evidence-free rebuttals inflate LLM scores by +0.53, and hostile or incomplete rebuttals can perversely help (Wang et al. 2026; Li et al. 2025).
12. **Stop on a rule and end with a human gate.** Accept a revision only if (a) the held-out pairwise judge prefers it beyond the noise floor and (b) the claim-evidence audit passes; otherwise revert (PaperOrchestra's accept/revert rule, made stricter). Stop after a fixed number of rounds or two rounds without a held-out gain, because proxy optimisation eventually hurts the gold objective (Gao et al. 2022). A human reads the final draft: automated scores did not predict human scores (6.1 vs 3.8; Agent Laboratory 2025).
